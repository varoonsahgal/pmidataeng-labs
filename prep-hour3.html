Understand it · 1 minute

The point

Every section below ends with a "Back to the point" box that ties what you just read to this one sentence.

A request is intent, not a specification.

Marketing asks for "real-time conversion dashboards in Power BI, across all our markets". It sounds clear. It is not: each of four ordinary words hides a guess, and two of those guesses are simply wrong. Build exactly what was asked and you get a project that cannot work, or one that breaks the law in two countries.

The habit this hour teaches: before anyone writes code, find the guesses, ask the few questions that change the build, and settle the arguments with a number from the warehouse. The theme has a name: shift left (moving the questions earlier, explained below).

105Mondays

Sales data arrives once a week, always on a Monday. "Real-time" was never possible.

0allowed in IT and ID

Under-25s we may market to in Italy and Indonesia. The law forbids it.

5questions

Claude drafts 15 to 20. Students keep only the five that change the build.

0lines of code

On purpose. The deliverable is the plan, not a pipeline.

Back to the point

Hours 1 and 2 caught a wrong guess after the code existed. Hour 3 catches the guesses before. Same mistake, much cheaper fix.

Understand it · 2 minutes

The story, and the PMI words

Four panels, then every company word in them, decoded.

The ask

Marketing: "Can you build us real-time conversion dashboards in Power BI, across all our markets, so we can watch the smoke-free transition as it happens?"

The temptation

An engineer could start building right away: a live data feed, one big chart for every country. It feels helpful and fast.

The catch

The data only arrives weekly. An under-25 view would be illegal in Italy and Indonesia. "Conversion" was never defined. Power BI is Desktop only.

The fix

Students unpack the four words, keep five questions, prove two answers with queries, and write the requirements nobody said.

The PMI words, decoded

Smoke-free transition PMI's big goal

PMI (Philip Morris International) wants adult smokers to switch from cigarettes to products that do not burn tobacco: IQOS (heats tobacco), VEEV (an e-cigarette) and ZYN (nicotine pouches). Marketing wants to "watch the transition as it happens".

Conversion one switch

A smoker switching to one of those products. In the data it is one of five stages in a customer's diary: registration → trial → conversion → retention or churn.

the request never says which stage counts, or per what period. That is one of the four guesses.

Market · JP, IT, ID a country

PMI calls each country a "market": JP Japan, IT Italy, ID Indonesia. Each has its own laws.

three schools in three countries with three different rulebooks. "One rule for all schools" might break one of the rulebooks.

Regulator the government watchdog

The government body that sets and enforces the rules for tobacco products in a country: MHLW in Japan (Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare), AAMS/EU in Italy (Italy's customs and monopolies agency, plus EU law), BPOM in Indonesia (its food and drug agency).

the regulator is the stakeholder everyone forgets to invite.

The under-25 rule a legal line

In Italy and Indonesia, PMI may not market to anyone aged 18 to 24. In Japan it may, if they said yes. In the data the age group is the column age_band, value '18-24'.

an "under-25 view across all markets" is illegal in two of the three.

Power BI · Desktop vs Service Microsoft's chart tool

Power BI is Microsoft's tool for building dashboards. Desktop is a program on one person's computer that makes a file. The Service is the paid online version that shares a dashboard and refreshes it automatically. Marketing has Desktop only.

Desktop is a Word document you email around; the Service is a shared Google Doc that updates itself.

Dashboard a page of charts

A screen of charts and numbers that people check regularly, like a car's dashboard shows speed and fuel.

Marketing named a dashboard. The real goal behind it is a decision they want to make.

Sell-through shop sales

What the shops (PMI's "distributors") sold to customers, reported once a week. Table rst.sell_through.

its weekly rhythm is what kills "real-time".

Back to the point

Notice none of these words is technical, yet every one changes what you would build. The request is the most dangerous code in the project, because nobody tests it.

Understand it · 2 minutes

The process words

Six words students use all hour, then the four bits of SQL in the two proofs. The basics guide covers tables, SQL and NULL.

Request vs specification wish vs blueprint

A request says what someone wants ("real-time"). A specification says exactly what will be built, so you can test it ("refreshed by 07:00 every morning").

"make me something nice for dinner" vs a recipe with quantities.

Stakeholder everyone with a stake

Anyone who asks for, uses, pays for or governs the thing. The one everybody forgets is the one who governs: Legal, the regulator, the owner of the consent data.

planning a school trip: students, teachers, parents… and the head teacher who has to sign the risk form.

Feasibility verdict can we build it?

The answer to one requirement. Only three kinds: buildable as asked buildable with a change not as asked, each with a number from the warehouse beside it.

there is no bare "no". "Not as asked" always comes with what can be built.

Functional vs non-functional what vs how

Functional: what it shows (weekly conversions per market). Non-functional: how it must behave (how fresh, who may see it, which law applies). Nobody says the second kind; everybody expects it.

ordering a pizza. Functional: large, pepperoni. Non-functional: hot, within 30 minutes, at the right address. Nobody says "hot, please".

Batch vs real-time the bus vs the taxi

Batch: data arrives in scheduled loads. Real-time: each event arrives within seconds. HORIZON is batch: once a week.

a newspaper delivered every Monday vs a live news feed.

As-of date the freshness label

The date the data was last loaded, printed next to every number, so nobody mistakes last week's number for today's.

the "best before" date on a milk carton.

group by one answer per group

"Split the rows into piles (one per market) and give me one answer per pile."

Proof B returns one row per market.

count(*) filter (where …) count only some

"Count only the rows that pass this test." Lets one query count everything and a subset side by side.

Proof A counts all rows and, next to it, only the non-Monday ones.

exists ( … ) is there at least one?

A yes/no question about another table: "does this person have at least one slip that says yes?" A person with three yes-slips still counts once.

Proof B counts people, not permission slips.

to_char(date, 'Dy') the day name

Turns a date into its short day name: Mon, Tue… <> 'Mon' means "is not Monday".

Proof A uses it to show no data ever arrives on a non-Monday.

Back to the point

Each process word is a tool for one job: turning intent (what someone wants) into a specification (what you will build and can test). The SQL words are there only to back the verdicts with numbers.

Understand it · 2 minutes

The trap, drawn

Each of the four words is a fork in the road. Left alone, the engineer silently picks a branch, usually the expensive or illegal one.

Why it is a trap

Nobody is lying or careless. Marketing pictures "numbers moving live" because that is what dashboards look like on TV. The engineer fills the gaps with the most likely guess. That is exactly what Claude did with "the consent record" in Hour 1. People and language models fail the same way on a vague request.

Back to the point

A request is intent. Each vague word is a fork, and the specification is the list of branches you chose on purpose, with the reason for each.

Understand it · 1 minute

What "shift left" means

Every project moves left to right. The further right a mistake is found, the more it costs to fix. "Shift left" means moving the questions toward the start.

HourThe unchecked guessWhere it was caughtWhat it cost
Hour 1"attach the consent record" (there were up to four)in review, 25 minutes after the drafta rewrite of one model
Hour 2"online shops are not stores, so drop them"in production, months later105 missing rows on every dashboard
Hour 3"real-time", "all markets"…now, before any codefive questions
Back to the point

The line to say: "What moves left is not the testing. It is the questions." Same kind of mistake in all three hours; three very different prices.

Back to the point

If one word gets answers from 1 to 86,400, the request is not a specification. That spread is the whole lesson of the hour.

Prove · SQLTools · 11 min · class 2:34

Two queries settle two arguments

2 min read
Students do

Run two queries in SQLTools, then fill a four-line feasibility gate: one verdict per requirement, each with a number.

They see

Proof A: 2730 | 105 | 0 | 2024-01-01 | 2025-12-29. Proof B: ID 191 · 0, IT 249 · 0, JP 359 · 319.

It means

"I don't think real-time is possible" starts an argument. "105 Mondays, zero exceptions" ends it. Demonstrate, don't assert.

Proof A · "How often does sales data actually arrive?" (real-time)

select count(*) as rows,
       count(distinct sale_date) as weeks,
       count(*) filter (where to_char(sale_date, 'Dy') <> 'Mon')
         as not_monday,
       min(sale_date) as first_week,
       max(sale_date) as last_week
from rst.sell_through;
LineIn plain EnglishAnswer
count(*) as rowshow many rows of sales in total?2,730
count(distinct sale_date) as weekshow many different dates? Each one is a delivery105
count(*) filter (where to_char(sale_date,'Dy') <> 'Mon')count only rows not dated on a Monday0
min(sale_date) / max(sale_date)first and last delivery2024-01-01 · 2025-12-29

Weekly, like clockwork. A live feed would show millions of rows, one every few seconds. Verdict: "real-time" is buildable with a change: fresh every morning, with the as-of date next to every number.

Proof B · "In each market, how many under-25s may we market to?" (all markets)

select c.market_code,
       count(*) as under25_people,

           select 1
           from mpl.consent_records m
           where m.consumer_id = c.consumer_id
             and m.marketing_allowed)) as allowed
from clr.consumers c
where c.age_band = '18-24'

order by c.market_code;

Specify · Sonnet · 8 min · class 2:45

Write the requirements nobody said

1 min read
Students do

Paste prompt 4a (same chat): Marketing's answers go in, two lists come out. Then curate: make sure the as-of date is there.

They see

A short functional list ("weekly conversion count per market, dated by its Monday…") and a table of non-functional requirements (refresh by 07:00, access, data residency, the under-25 rule, a visible missed load). Claude often forgets the as-of date.

It means

Answers in a chat are easy to forget. A short written list is what both sides sign, and what the build is later tested against. That is the specification.

Functional · what it shows
  • weekly conversions per market (JP, IT, ID), each week dated by its Monday
  • week-on-week change; a flag when a market falls two weeks running
  • markets side by side, never one blended total
  • no age breakdown below 25 outside Japan
Non-functional · how it behaves
  • refreshed by 07:00 each morning
  • access: Marketing analytics team only
  • data residency: JP and ID are APAC, IT is EU
  • a missed load is visible (Hour 2: green ≠ right)
  • an as-of date on every number

Why the as-of date matters, drawn as a week in the life of the dashboard:

Dashboard refreshedNewest sales it can showWhat a reader assumes

"Refreshed each morning" describes the report. The as-of date describes the data.

The AI connection

Requirements for humans are context for models. A vague request to a person ("build me a real-time dashboard") and a vague prompt to Claude ("attach the consent record", Hour 1) fail the same way: the gap is filled with the most likely guess, silently. That is why every prompt today has Context, Tables and Rules before the ask.

Be ready · 3 minutes

If a student asks…

The questions that actually come up, with answers you can say out loud.

"Isn't saying 'not real-time' just being negative?"

No: every verdict comes with what can be built ("fresh every morning, as-of date on every number"). And it is backed by a fact: data arrives on 105 Mondays and zero other days. A dashboard cannot be fresher than its data.

"Couldn't we just buy a streaming platform?"

You could stream the dashboard, but the shops still report once a week. You would pay for a live pipe carrying weekly data. That is the Vendor's role card in Step 3: ask them for the number that shows their data is faster.

"Why is Japan allowed and Italy not? Is the data wrong?"

Different laws. Japan permits marketing to adults aged 18 to 24 who said yes; Italy and Indonesia forbid it. The data obeys: 0 of 249 in Italy, 0 of 191 in Indonesia, 319 of 359 in Japan. Zero here is correct, not a bug.

"Why count people and not consent rows?"

Each person has several permission slips (one per channel). Counting slips counts some people up to four times: Hour 1's fan-out again. exists asks "at least one yes?" so each person counts once. Older slides quoted 73%, which counted slips; people is the honest unit.

"Isn't 'conversion' obvious?"

Ask the room for a definition and you will get several: the conversion stage, trial-to-conversion rate, conversions per campaign, per day or per week. Each is a different query and a different chart. That is why it is question 1.

"What's wrong with Power BI Desktop?"

Nothing, as long as everyone knows what it is: a file on one computer that someone refreshes and sends. It is not a shared, self-updating dashboard. So the questions become: who refreshes it, where is it stored, and who may see it?

"Who 'governs' this? I've never met them."

Legal / Regulatory for each market, and the owner of the consent data. They do not use the dashboard, but they can stop it. Claude usually leaves them off the stakeholder list, which is exactly why students check for them.

"Why Sonnet all hour, no Opus?"

The hard part this hour is judgment: which questions matter, which verdict is fair. That judgment belongs to the students. Claude only generates options, which the cheaper model does fine.

"What if Marketing insists on real-time anyway?"

Show the number (105 Mondays) and the offer (fresh each morning). If they still want it, that is now a funded project to get the shops to report more often. It is a business decision with a price, made on purpose instead of by accident.

Hour 3 · Shift left

A request is intent, not a specification. Ask the questions, and prove with a number, before anyone builds.

  2. Clarify: Claude drafts 20 questions; keep the 5 where a different answer changes the build.
  3. Prove: 105 Mondays and 0 other days; under-25s allowed IT 0, ID 0, JP 319 of 359. Four verdicts, each with a number.
  4. Specify: functional and non-functional requirements, including the as-of date nobody asked for.
