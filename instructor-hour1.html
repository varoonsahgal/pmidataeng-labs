Understand it · 1 minute

The hour in one breath

  1. The request: "Claude drafted the Italy conversions-with-consent model in a minute. It runs. Can we ship it?"
  2. The catch: the consent table has several rows per person, and the prompt never said which one counts. So the draft guesses, and every guess runs without an error.
  3. What students do: draft it with Claude (Sonnet), prove it with three checks they already know the answer to (794 rows · consumer 3552 gets true · 0 unknowns), have Opus plan the fix and Sonnet build it, then lock it with 4 dbt tests.
  4. What they leave with: a tested dbt model, analytics.italy_conversion_events: 794 rows, one per conversion, each carrying the consent that was in force on the day.

The line to repeat all hour: the assistant drafts, the warehouse decides, you sign.

Understand it · 3 minutes

The three tables, simply

Only three tables matter this hour. Two are joined in the model; the third only appears in the last test.

clr.lifecycle_events 794 Italy conversions

What it is: a diary of what each consumer did. One row = one step in their journey: registration → trial → conversion → retention → churn.

What we use: only the rows where market_code = 'IT' and lifecycle_stage = 'conversion'. That is 794 rows, and 794 different people (nobody in Italy converts twice).

Like a sales ledger: one line per thing that happened, with a date.

mpl.consent_records 2.51 rows per person

What it is: marketing permission slips. One row = one consumer's yes or no for one channel (email, sms, push or postal), with the date it was recorded.

The trap: 15,052 rows for 6,000 people. Each person has 1 to 4 slips, so consumer_id is not unique here.

Like a drawer of signed permission slips. The question is always: which slip was in the drawer on the day?

clr.consumers 6,000 people

What it is: who each consumer is. One row per person, including age_band.

Where it shows up: only in Step 4, in the under-25 test. Italian law forbids marketing to 18 to 24 year olds, so a correct model never marks an Italian '18-24' consumer as allowed.

Like the customer master file: one row per person, safe to join.

The two columns that decide everything

event_date on the diary (when they converted) and consent_date on the slip (when the yes or no was recorded). Both are type date. The whole lab is about comparing those two dates correctly.

Understand it · 20 minutes, the night before

The problem, in plain words

If you can explain consumer 3552, you can teach this hour. This section walks through 3552 slowly, using every real row, and then covers the three edge cases that sit behind the numbers 8, 32 and 5. Every row shown here was queried from the HORIZON warehouse on 2026-10-25.

The whole problem in four sentences

Marketing wants one yes/no per Italy conversion: "on the day this person converted, were we allowed to contact them?" The answer lives in a table of permission slips, and most people have several slips (one per channel, signed on different days). So for every conversion the SQL must pick exactly one slip, and it must be the right one: the newest slip that already existed on the conversion day. Every way of getting that wrong still runs without an error, which is the point of the hour.

2 · The drawer, day by day

The single most useful picture: imagine the slips go into a drawer on the day they are signed. On any given day, the drawer holds only the slips signed on or before that day, and the newest slip in the drawer is the current answer. The answer for the same person changes over time:

DateWhat happenedSlips in the drawer that dayNewest slip in the drawerAllowed that day?
26 Oct 2024registration (event 9537)nonenoneunknown → false
10 Nov 2024email slip signed (yes); trial (event 9538)emailemail, truetrue
16 Nov 2024push slip signed (yes)email, pushpush, truetrue
17 Nov 2024conversion (event 9539)email, pushpush, truetrue ← the answer we need
19 Nov 2024postal slip signed (withdrawn)email, push, postalpostal, falsefalse
27 Dec 2024churn (event 9540)email, push, postalpostal, falsefalse

So "is 3552 allowed?" has no single answer. It was unknown in October, true from 10 to 18 November, and false from 19 November on. The model answers it for one specific day per row, the conversion day. Today's answer (false) is the wrong one for a row dated 17 November. That is what "point-in-time" means: use what was true at that point in time, not what is true now.

Why it matters outside the classroom

Suppose a regulator asks in March 2025: "on 17 November you sent 3552 an offer. Were you allowed to?" The honest answer is yes: they had said yes on push the day before. A model that says false is wrong in the safe direction for 3552, but the same mistake runs the other way for other people (below: 8 people marked allowed who were not). A model that cannot answer "what did we know on the day" fails the first question in an audit.

3 · Three drafts, traced row by row for 3552

Here is what the database actually does with 3552 under each of the three common draft shapes. Read the middle column like a slow-motion replay.

Draft A · the plain join (the classic mistake)

from lifecycle_events e
left join consent_records m
    on m.consumer_id = e.consumer_id      -- nothing else
  1. The database takes event 9539 and looks for every slip where consumer_id = 3552. It finds three.
  2. A join pairs the event with every match, so it writes three output rows, one per slip:
event_idevent_dateslip joined onmarketing_allowed
95392024-11-17email · 10 Novtrue
95392024-11-17push · 16 Novtrue
95392024-11-17postal · 19 Novfalse

Result for 3552: 3 rows, two answers. One conversion now looks like three conversions, and it is both allowed and not allowed. Across Italy: each of the 794 people is copied once per slip they own: 197×1 + 195×2 + 207×3 + 195×4 = 1,988 rows. A report counting "allowed" rows would say 1,226, which is more people than Italy has conversions. This is a fan-out: the join silently changed what one row means (from "one conversion" to "one conversion × one slip"). Caught by: check 1 (1,988), check 2 (3 rows) and the unique test (FAIL 597).

Why LEFT vs INNER does not matter here

Every Italy converter has at least one slip, so a plain INNER join also gives exactly 1,988. LEFT and INNER only differ for people with no matching slip, and with no date condition nobody is unmatched.

Draft B · "newest slip on file" (the sneaky one)

select distinct on (e.event_id) e.event_id, m.marketing_allowed
from lifecycle_events e
join consent_records m on m.consumer_id = e.consumer_id   -- no date
order by e.event_id, m.consent_date desc
  1. Join: as in draft A, 9539 is paired with all three slips (3 rows).
  2. Sort those three rows newest first by consent_date: postal (19 Nov), push (16 Nov), email (10 Nov).
  3. Keep the first row per event (distinct on): postal, 19 Nov, false.

Result for 3552: 1 row · false. One row, so the count looks perfect, but the slip it used did not exist on 17 November. It was signed two days later. This draft looks more careful than draft A (it deduplicates), which is exactly why it is dangerous.

Across Italy: 31 conversions get a slip signed after the conversion. For 18 of them the later slip happens to say the same thing, so the answer is right by luck. For 13 the answer flips:

GroupPeopleWhoWhat went wrong
true on the day → false53552, 3658, 3996, 4046, 4945a later withdrawal or denial is applied backwards. 3552 is one of these.
false on the day → true23861, 4613a later yes is applied backwards: marked allowed when they were not
no slip yet → true63068, 3311, 3456, 3887, 4036, 4514they had no slip at all on the day; a yes signed later is used

So 8 people (2 + 6) are marked allowed when they were not. That is the dangerous direction: contacting people without permission. Caught by: check 2 only. Checks 1 and 3 pass (794 rows, 0 NULLs), and all four dbt tests pass. Challenge 1 makes students see this; Challenge 2's test catches it (FAIL 13).

Draft C · "newest slip on or before the day" (correct)

select distinct on (e.event_id) e.event_id, m.marketing_allowed
from lifecycle_events e
join consent_records m on m.consumer_id = e.consumer_id
                      and m.consent_date <= e.event_date   -- the extra line
order by e.event_id, m.consent_date desc, m.consent_id desc
  1. Join, with the date condition: 9539 (17 Nov) is paired only with slips dated on or before 17 Nov. Email (10 Nov) qualifies, push (16 Nov) qualifies, postal (19 Nov) is filtered out before anything else happens. Two rows.
  2. Sort newest first: push (16 Nov), then email (10 Nov).
  3. Keep the first row: push, 16 Nov, true.

Result for 3552: 1 row · true. The whole difference between draft B and draft C is one line: and m.consent_date <= e.event_date. It is the line Challenge 1 asks students to comment out.

4 · The 8 with no slip yet (why 786, why NULL, why false)

Draft C has one more problem. For some people, every slip was signed after they converted. On their conversion day the drawer was empty. These are all 8 of them:

event_idconsumer_idconvertedtheir slips (all signed later)
827230682024-09-25sms true 2024-10-02

Follow consumer 4036 through draft C step by step:

  1. The join with the date condition looks for 4036's slips dated on or before 20 May 2024. The only slip is 28 May, so it finds nothing.
  2. What happens next depends on the join type. An INNER join drops event 10762 entirely: the conversion disappears from the model. A LEFT join keeps the event and fills marketing_allowed with NULL (empty, unknown).
  3. With an INNER join the model has 786 rows: 8 conversions gone, no error. With a LEFT join it has 794 rows, of which 8 are NULL.

Why NULL is dangerous, with real counts. Run these on a draft that left the 8 as NULL:

QueryAnswer
where marketing_allowed = true472
where marketing_allowed = false314
allowed + not allowed786, not 794
where marketing_allowed is null8: the ones that fell through both filters

In SQL, comparing NULL with anything (NULL = true, NULL = false) gives "unknown", and where keeps only rows that are definitely true. So the 8 vanish from both reports, and nobody sees them. That is why check 3 uses is null: = null never matches anything.

The fix: coalesce(marketing_allowed, false) means "use the answer, and if it is empty use false". After it: 472 allowed + 322 not allowed (314 + the 8) = 794.

The question you will get: "but 4036 said yes eight days later, so why false?"

Because on 20 May we did not have that yes. Consent has to exist, and be provable, at the moment you use it (that is how GDPR works in Italy). "We had no permission on file" is the same as "not allowed", not "probably fine". The later yes would count for a conversion on or after 28 May. The comment in the SQL (-- no consent recorded yet = unknown, and unknown is not "allowed") is the written record of that decision, which is why the plan prompt insists on it.

A subtle point for the reference model

The reference model uses an INNER join inside the consent_on_the_day CTE, and sharp students ask "doesn't that drop the 8?" Yes, deliberately: the CTE is the list of events that have a slip on the day (786). The outer left join brings the other 8 back with NULL, and coalesce turns NULL into false. INNER inside, LEFT outside, coalesce on top. See How the correct SQL works.

5 · Same-day slips: why <= and not <

5 Italy conversions have a slip dated the same day as the conversion. The dates have no time of day, so we cannot tell which came first. Our contract says a slip dated on the conversion day counts, hence <=.

event_idconsumer_iddatesame-day slip
829130752024-11-03email true
1066239962024-09-19sms true (their only slip on or before the day)
1133342602024-05-01push false
1148743242025-07-15postal true
1190644862024-10-23push false

If a draft writes <, four of these still come out the same (an earlier slip gives the same answer), but consumer 3996 loses their only slip. How it shows up: check 3 reads 9 instead of 8 before the coalesce. After the coalesce the draft reads 794 · true · 0 and passes all four tests while being wrong for one person. If a student reports 9 NULLs, this is the cause.

6 · Two slips on the deciding day: why the consent_id tie-break

For 32 conversions, the newest qualifying date has two slips on it. For 9 of those the two slips disagree. Example, consumer 3390, conversion event 9120 on 3 Aug 2025:

consent_idcomm_channelmarketing_allowedconsent_dateon 3 Aug…
8504smstrue2025-06-04older
8506emailtrue2025-06-06older
8503pushfalse2025-06-23newest date, lower id
8505postaltrue2025-06-23newest date, higher id: wins → true

Three things can happen, depending on the draft:

  1. No tie-break (order by e.event_id, m.consent_date desc only): Postgres keeps whichever of the two rows it happens to read first. The answer for those 9 people can change between runs or machines. So if two students with the same draft report slightly different results in a values comparison, this is why.
  2. max(consent_date) then join back on that date: both rows match the max date, so the event appears twice. 794 + 32 = 826 rows; unique fails with 32.
  3. With the tie-break (…, m.consent_id desc): always 8505, always true. Same answer every run.

Be honest about what the tie-break is. "Higher consent_id wins" is not "the later one wins": we just saw that consent_id is not in time order. It is an arbitrary but stable rule, chosen so the output is deterministic. The truly right answer for a same-day conflict is a business question (should a "no" win over a "yes"?), which is another Hour 3 bridge.

7 · The channel question (if a sharp student asks)

Our rule ignores channel: the newest slip on any channel decides. A student may object: "3552 withdrew postal consent on 19 Nov. Why should that stop emails?" They are right that it is debatable. What to say:

  • For 3552 on 17 Nov it does not matter. Email said yes (10 Nov) and push said yes (16 Nov); postal did not exist yet. Every possible rule gives true, so check 2 is safe from this debate.
  • Across Italy it matters a lot. For 278 of the 794 conversions, the person's channels disagree on the day. Campaign 1 was an email campaign: 45 of its 74 conversions are allowed under the any-channel rule, only 29 using email slips alone. 16 people would be emailed without an email yes.
  • It is not a SQL question. The owner of the consent data decides the rule, ideally before anyone writes SQL. Today we follow the contract we were given (any channel). That is Challenge 3 and the whole of Hour 3: shift left.

8 · The 90-second version for the room

Say, pointing at the 3552 tables on screen

"This is consumer 3552. They converted on 17 November. They have three permission slips: email yes on the 10th, push yes on the 16th, postal withdrawn on the 19th."

"Question: on the 17th, were we allowed to contact them? Look at the drawer on the 17th. The postal slip is not in it yet; it arrives two days later. The newest slip in the drawer is push, yes. So: true."

"Now three drafts. Glue on every slip: one conversion becomes three rows, two say yes, one says no. Take the newest slip on file: postal, no. Wrong, because that slip did not exist yet. Take the newest slip on or before the 17th: push, yes. Right."

"All three run green. The only way to know which one you got is to ask the table about someone whose answer you already know. That person is 3552, event 9539, and the answer is true."

Why the AI gets it wrong (and why that is not its fault)

The prompt says "attach each event's marketing_allowed from the consent record". There is no single consent record; there are up to four. Claude only sees the prompt, so it guesses. Modern models often guess right (see If your demo draft comes back right). The lesson is not "the AI is bad". It is: you cannot tell which guess you got by reading. You find out by counting.

Understand it · 10 minutes

How the correct SQL works

The reference model from the student guide's Stuck? panel, with 3552 and 4036 traced through every part. You will be asked about at least one of these lines.

-- italy_conversion_events: one row per Italy conversion event,
-- with the marketing permission in force on the event date.
{{ config(materialized='view', schema='analytics') }}

-- PART 1: the 794 conversions
with events as (
    select event_id, consumer_id, market_code, product_code, lifecycle_stage, event_date, campaign_id
    from {{ source('clr', 'lifecycle_events') }}
    where market_code = 'IT'
      and lifecycle_stage = 'conversion'
),

-- PART 2: one slip per event (786 events have one)
consent_on_the_day as (
    -- one row per event: the newest consent dated on or before the event
    select distinct on (e.event_id)
        e.event_id,
        m.marketing_allowed
    from events e
    join {{ source('mpl', 'consent_records') }} m
        on m.consumer_id = e.consumer_id
       and m.consent_date <= e.event_date
    order by e.event_id, m.consent_date desc, m.consent_id desc
)

-- PART 3: every event, with its slip or false
select
    e.event_id, e.consumer_id, e.market_code, e.product_code,
    e.lifecycle_stage, e.event_date, e.campaign_id,
    -- no consent recorded yet = unknown, and unknown is not "allowed"
    coalesce(c.marketing_allowed, false) as marketing_allowed
from events e
left join consent_on_the_day c using (event_id)

The same rule in other shapes (Claude may write any of these)

Students' fixed models will differ. These two and the reference above are all correct, and all give 794 · true · 0 (with the coalesce). Accept any of them; check the date condition, the tie-break and the coalesce, not the shape.

-- Shape 2: row_number(). Works on Snowflake, BigQuery, Databricks too, not just Postgres.
ranked as (
    select e.event_id, m.marketing_allowed,
           row_number() over (partition by e.event_id
                              order by m.consent_date desc, m.consent_id desc) as rn
    from events e
    join {{ source('mpl', 'consent_records') }} m
      on m.consumer_id = e.consumer_id and m.consent_date <= e.event_date
)
-- then: left join ranked c on c.event_id = e.event_id and c.rn = 1

-- Shape 3: a lateral join (current Claude drafts often do this)
from events e
left join lateral (
    select m.marketing_allowed
    from {{ source('mpl', 'consent_records') }} m
    where m.consumer_id = e.consumer_id and m.consent_date <= e.event_date
    order by m.consent_date desc, m.consent_id desc
    limit 1
) c on true
The row_number gotcha

With row_number, the rn = 1 condition must go in the on clause of the outer LEFT join (or in a filtered CTE). If it goes in the outer where, the 8 NULL rows have rn NULL, get filtered out, and the model drops to 786.

The curly brackets, for when someone asks

  • {{ config(materialized='view', schema='analytics') }}: "build this as a view in schema analytics". A view stores the query, not the data; every time you select from it, Postgres re-runs the query. That is why dbt run takes a second and why the checks always see the latest SQL.
  • {{ source('clr', 'lifecycle_events') }}: becomes "horizon"."clr"."lifecycle_events". It only works if a sources file declares clr (pre-flight 2b); otherwise "depends on a source named … which was not found".
  • {{ ref('italy_conversion_events') }} (used in the tests): becomes "horizon"."analytics"."italy_conversion_events", and tells dbt the test depends on the model.
  • To see the plain SQL dbt actually ran: target/compiled/horizon_dbt/models/analytics/italy_conversion_events.sql. Useful when a student's error message is confusing.

What this table teaches you (and what to say)

  1. Check 1 is the only thing that catches shape D. An INNER join gives 786 rows, a true for 3552, no NULLs, and passes all four tests. If someone reports 786, say: "Eight conversions disappeared and every test is green. Only the row count saw it."
  2. Check 2 is the only thing among the four tests and three checks that catches shape B. This is the headline of Step 4: "none of the four tests catches the newest-on-file draft".
  3. The under-25 test passes on every draft except I. Italy has 249 consumers aged 18 to 24 (93 of them converted), and every one of their 624 slips already says marketing_allowed = false. So no draft that copies marketing_allowed can break the law here. Do not tell the room the under-25 test "caught" anything. Say: "It is a tripwire. It fires the day someone switches to consent_status, or a bad slip is loaded." Shape I proves it: 71 under-25s marked allowed, because "granted" ignores the age gate.
  4. The rows marked in amber pass all three checks and all four tests and are still wrong. Check 2 tests one person, and 3552 happens to come out true under G, H and I. This is the honest limit of the method: three checks catch the common mistakes, not every mistake. The defences are (a) reading the plan for the date rule, the tie-break and the coalesce, which is why the Step 3 checklist exists, and (b) Challenge 2's values test, which checks every row the way check 2 checks one. If a student's plan says "any consent granted", "highest consent_id" or "consent_status", reject it even if their checks look perfect.
Why unique says 597 and not 1,194 or 1,988

dbt's unique test returns one row per value that appears more than once, not one row per copy. 597 event_ids are duplicated (195 people with 2 slips + 207 with 3 + 195 with 4). The 197 people with one slip are not duplicated, so they are not counted. Same logic for shape E: 32 event_ids appear twice, so FAIL 32.

The reference corrected model

The same model as the guide's Stuck? panel. Paste it, dbt run, and the checks read 794 · true · 0. On your Mac, make s1-fixed installs it with the 4 tests.

-- italy_conversion_events: grain = ONE ROW PER CONVERSION EVENT (event_id).
-- Consent is point-in-time: the latest consent row dated on or before the event.
-- A missing/unknown permission is treated as NOT allowed (coalesce to false).
{{ config(materialized='view', schema='analytics') }}

with events as (
    select event_id, consumer_id, market_code, product_code, lifecycle_stage, event_date, campaign_id
    from {{ source('clr', 'lifecycle_events') }}
    where market_code = 'IT'
      and lifecycle_stage = 'conversion'
),

consent_as_of_event as (
    -- exactly one consent row per event, resolved in a single pass
    select distinct on (e.event_id)
        e.event_id,
        m.marketing_allowed
    from events e
    join {{ source('mpl', 'consent_records') }} m
        on m.consumer_id = e.consumer_id
       and m.consent_date <= e.event_date
    order by e.event_id, m.consent_date desc, m.consent_id desc
)

select
    e.event_id,
    e.consumer_id,
    e.market_code,
    e.product_code,
    e.lifecycle_stage,
    e.event_date,
    e.campaign_id,
    -- no consent recorded yet = unknown, and unknown is not "allowed"
    coalesce(c.marketing_allowed, false) as marketing_allowed
from events e
left join consent_as_of_event c using (event_id)
